I began identifying as vegan on January 1st, 2024. Now that my 2-year Veganiversary has passed, I'd like to mark the occasion by answering some questions & talking points that regularly come up whenever I discuss this somewhat uncommon "lifestyle" that I've adopted.

As a vegan, you find yourself having the same tedious conversations about veganism over and over again. It's the equivalent of a tall person being told "wow, you're tall" every time they meet someone new. For this reason, I hesitate to reveal to anyone that I'm vegan, but it tends to be dragged out into the open when food is being eaten or discussed. So, it's good to have my answers straight for when that inevitably happens.

This is a living document and will be updated as new questions and better answers occur to me.

> This is my vegan FAQ. There are many like it, but this one is mine.

### What is veganism?
Here's the Vegan Society's definition of veganism. I'll be referencing it later.

> Veganism is a philosophy and way of living which seeks to exclude - as far as is possible and practicable - all forms of exploitation of, and cruelty to, animals for food, clothing or any other purpose.

In the popular consciousness, veganism is a diet: vegans don't consume animal products like meat, milk, cheese and eggs. However, the above definition also encompasses other forms of exploitation, such as making clothing from animal skin, and animal-based entertainment like horse-racing.

### Why are you vegan?
Mostly for the animals. Also for the environment (more on that later, also). I've read that it has health benefits (eating meat causes higher levels of cholesterol and increases cancer risk, apparently?), but I generally don't make life choices based on small negative impacts to my health - I still drink alcohol, for example.

### How did you become vegan?
(This answer is more of a personal narrative, and quite rambly, so feel free to skip it).

For a long time, I had the vague sense that my meat-eating habit wasn't based on a particularly solid moral foundation. The difference in the treatment of "pets" and "farm animals" is probably contradictory, I thought. And in the future, when all our nutritional needs are met by lab-produced pills, and we can experience any possible sensory delight through the control of our brain interfaces, then surely the habit of killing animals and eating their meat will be considered a barbaric and pointlessly cruel practice. The vegetarians and vegans, I said to myself, are on the right side of history.

But there's a difference between having a vague awareness of something, and feeling the need to act on it. I was comfortable in my decades-long custom of eating meat. I liked eggs in all their forms: scrambled, fried, poached, or in an omelette. Chocolate and ice-cream were my sweets of choice. Cheese pizza was perhaps my favourite food to eat at restaurants, and I'd developed a taste for proper stonebaked ones. I happily drank standalone glasses of milk, like any good Irish boy. As for steak - "medium, please!". I didn't spare much thought for how these foods were produced.

The turning point was when I returned to university and suddenly found myself surrounded by vegetarians and vegans. None of them ever tried to proselythise me, but through observing them and through casual lunchtime conversation, I became conscious of my decades-long habit, and of the fact that I could change it if I wanted to.

Then, somewhat whimsically, and without much moral conviction behind it, I took up the challenge of cooking more vegetarian meals at home. It seemed like a generally *good* thing to do. Good for the planet, good for animals, and probably good for my health. So... why not!

To begin with, I swapped out ingredients in my existing recipes. For example, my beef stir fry became a vegetable stir fry. Beans and lentils were adopted into my roster of ingredients. I learned to make chickpea curry. Before long, meat ended up being entirely eliminated from my cooking, and my diet was ~95% vegetarian. I was reluctant to commit to full vegetarianism, as I didn't want to limit my options when eating out at restaurants. There was a honey-glazed chicken burger that I was especially attached to. Besides that, I was scared to apply a definitive label like  "vegetarian" to myself, which would make it harder to change course (I'm rigid like that); and I didn't want to inconvenience other people when they cooked for me.

The next step in my journey was that I started dating a vegan. "Something you should probably know is that I'm 95% vegetarian," I told her during our first ever conversation - thinking, based on her naturey vibes, that it would impress her. "Oh, that's okay, I'm vegan," she replied, unimpressed. She introduced me to oat milk, which soon became the only type of milk I drank, because it was tastier than cow milk. She also introduced me to tofu, which became yet another "meat substitute" in my diet. And, she brought me to lots of vegan-friendly restaurants. Gradually, I convinced myself that it was okay to let go of the final 5%.

I'm not sure when was the last time I intentionally ate meat. I remember there was an International Lunch in my university department, where each student would bring in a dish from their culture, and I decided to partake of the meat-based dishes. After the fact, however, I felt grossed out by what I had done. Eating meat no longer seemed like a normal thing to do. It wasn't compartmentalised like it was before, and I found it hard not to think of animals as I ate.



* Books and documentaries: Dominion. Eating Animals. Cadáver Exquisito.
* Visceral reaction to meat. Decompartmentalised. I don't yet have that reaction to animal products.
* Fully vegan at home. Occasionally consumed dairy products in restaurants.
* Kept putting off full veganism. "I want to be more informed on the dairy industry first", "it's theoretically possible to have ethical eggs", anticipating pushback, general inertia.
* Finally... Veganuary 2024.

### What do you eat?
Generally, I eat "normal" stuff like pasta, curry, stir fry, etc. - just without meat. For protein I eat beans, chickpeas, lentils and tofu. I also drink oat milk instead of cow milk. It's actually more insightful to ask what I *don't* eat, since I've had to give up a lot of sweet things (most chocolate; most ice-cream; and most gummy sweets, since they often contain gelatine (animal fat)) and cheese (I've only tried 1 vegan cheese brand that tasted *really* legit).

I've uploaded some of the recipes that I cook [here](/recipes.html). 

### "Wow, that must be so hard, I could never do that"
Not really. It doesn't require any extra effort in my day-to-day life, besides checking ingredients lists when shopping.

When eating out, I sometimes have to go out of my way to find a suitable restaurant, but most places in Ireland have vegan-friendly options (except in small towns or old-fashioned pubs). I haven't had any trouble finding something to eat when traveling, but again, it's definitely easier in urban areas.

The main challenge has actually been friction with other people. My family hasn't been very accepting of my new diet, for example.

### Don't you need protein and calcium to survive?
From what I've read, calcium isn't really needed much beyond childhood, and there are lots of non-meat sources of protein. The things you actually need to look out for are iron and vitamin B12. I've started taking a supplement for the former, and for the latter I eat nutritional yeast (which is actually a nice addition to a sandwich, or to sprinkle on top of some dinners).

### Would you recommend veganism?
Ultimately, I do think it's a more ethical way to live. But I also believe people should be free to make their own decisions. If your preference is to continue eating meat, then my challenge is for you to learn more about where that meat comes from etc. If you're still fine with it, then that's grand. If you're uncomfortable even at the thought of learning more, then maybe that's your conscience speaking to you.
